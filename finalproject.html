Introduction

In the following report I will use data tidying and ggplot functions to show the relationship between wait times in the emergency room in every state in the United States and the condition in which patients arrive. I am hopeful to come to a conclusion on whether there are states that are better with response times as well as if some states are better at certain types of admissions and worse at others. Before every graph I will have a little bit of a description of what I am showcasing so that there is more clarity. At the end I will analyze what results I got and what they mean.

A Little About The Data

This data set comes from the TidyTuesday data set “Timely and Effective Care by US State”. This data comes from 2023 and 2024 and shows the average wait times for every US State as well as Washington DC and Puerto Rico. For this document, Puerto Rico will be excluded from the graphs, as my goal is to just highlight the United States and the wait times for all 50 states as well as Washington DC. The data also has percentages on vaccinations and how many people get care in an adequate amount of time when they are being admitted to the ER with symptoms of a stroke.

There are quite a few NAs in the care_state.score column that have the number 5 in the care_state.footnote column. When I look at the footnotes on the github page it tells me that the number 5 correlates to “Results are not available for this reporting period.” With this being known, I will remove all of the NAs that have 5 in the footnote column.

care_state_clean <- care_state %>% filter(!(is.na(score) & footnote == 5))

Now that the data has no more NAs, I am ready to begin tidying the data to showcase the relationship between patients’ care state and the average wait time.

Analysis

So what can we take away from these results? There are a couple more tests that I need to run to get a conclusive final answer to my question “are states slow in just one area of the emergency room or are they slow all around?” I need to run a correlation test and then a regression test on the ER wait times and the brain stroke results. This analysis will allow me to see the relation between these and come to a conclusion afterwards on whether or not long average wait times in the ER correlate to not receiving brain scan results in an adequate amount of time and whether or not patients should feel safe about having to get such important results in states where ER wait times are slower than normal.

After running this correlation test between the percentage of Brain stroke patients that received their results within 45 minutes and the average ER wait time, we got a correlation score of -0.35 rounded. This represents a mild negative correlation, meaning that as the average wait time of the ER goes up, the average percentage of patients receiving decreases. This is what I expected to happen, so I will run a regression test to see just how much these two variables are correlated to each other. This will give a clearer idea of just how much they are affected by one another and give me a time to present and not just a number.

After analyzing the data above, we can see a significant negative relation between the two variables. Looking at the slope, which is 0.069, this means that for every extra minute that the ER takes to help patients, about 0.069% of brain patients do not receive results in an adequate amount of time. This meant that for every 15 minutes 1% of patients are not receiving their potential brain stroke results within 45 minutes. That is a significant number of patients and is a concerning correlation between the two.

Conclusion

In conclusion, there is quite a slow response time in emergency rooms for any state, but the Northeast seems to struggle the most. This shows in all of the results, from having lower than usual brain stroke results within 45 minutes, having lower amounts of Covid vaccinations, and having a longer wait time for seeing a mental health professional in the ER. The analysis section brought this to light even more with the introduction of another map graph showing that the same states that are in lighter colors on the first graph (longer wait time) are in darker colors (lower percentage) on the second graph. This along with all of the other graphs introduced in the exploratory data analysis as well as the comments that have been made under each graph throughout have all been in an effort to show that there are relations to states that have longer ER wait times are slower in all facets of the emergency room, and they are not just lacking in one area. This is a concerning discovery, and hopefully there are more efforts made to these states that are lacking, but until then we have to work with what we are given.
